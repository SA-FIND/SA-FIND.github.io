The Electrowinning Process for Copper A Technical Report on Principles, Cell Design, Operation, and Practice 1. Introduction Copper electrowinning (EW) is an electrochemical process used to recover metallic copper from a purified, copper-bearing sulfate solution by passing a direct electric current through the solution between an insoluble anode and a cathode, causing dissolved copper ions to be reduced and deposited as solid metal on the cathode (Ti-Better, 2025). It is the final step of the hydrometallurgical route to copper metal, most commonly practised in conjunction with heap or dump leaching of oxide and low-grade ores followed by solvent extraction (SX), a combination widely referred to as SX-EW (KGHM, no date; Metso, no date a). Solvent extraction and electrowinning together account for a significant share of primary copper production worldwide. Wikipedia (no date), citing Bartos (2002), reports that SX-EW accounts for approximately 20% of worldwide copper production, alongside pyrometallurgical smelting and electrorefining of sulfide-derived anode copper, which together still supply the majority of global output (TSFX, no date). SAIMM (2002) similarly frames electrowinning as complementary to, rather than a replacement for, conventional smelter-refinery production. 1.1 Historical Development Hydrometallurgical recovery of copper from leach solutions has a long history: 911Metallurgist (no date b) traces large-scale leaching and cementation (precipitation of copper using scrap iron) back to Rio Tinto, Spain, around 1752, a practice that persisted in the Western United States well into the twentieth century. The modern SX-EW combination, however, is a product of the 1960s. According to SAIMM (2002), the key selective copper extractant (a hydroxyoxime reagent) was introduced around 1965, and the first commercial leach/solvent-extraction/electrowinning operation, the Bluebird plant of Ranchers Exploration and Development Corporation, began operating in March 1968. Prasad, Kenyen and Assar (1992) note that subsequent decades saw successive generations of extractant reagents with improved copper selectivity, faster phase-separation kinetics and reduced organic losses, alongside parallel improvements in mixer-settler and electrowinning cell design. Bartos (2002) analyses this trajectory using a technology-cycle framework, observing that SX-EW technology progressed from an initial complementary role alongside froth flotation to become a dominant, standalone route for treating oxide and secondary sulfide ores that would otherwise be uneconomic to process. From the modest beginnings of a single plant in 1968, installed SX-EW capacity had grown to roughly 2.8 million tonnes of copper per year by the early 2000s (SAIMM, 2002). This report describes the fundamental electrochemistry of copper electrowinning, the design and layout of a typical electrowinning cell (illustrated with a labelled diagram), the industrial process flow from leach solution to finished cathode, key operating parameters, common problems encountered in tankhouse operation, industrial case studies, recent technological advances, and the environmental and economic significance of the technology. All technical claims are supported by references to the metallurgical and patent literature cited throughout and listed, in Harvard style, in the reference list at the end of the report. 2. Fundamental Electrochemical Principles Electrowinning belongs to the broader family of electrometallurgical processes in which an external electrical power source drives a non-spontaneous reduction reaction, as opposed to a galvanic cell in which a spontaneous reaction generates current (Jiang et al., 2024). In copper electrowinning, direct current is passed between a permanent, essentially inert anode and a cathode immersed in an acidic copper sulfate electrolyte (ScienceDirect Topics, no date). 2.1 Electrode Reactions At the cathode, cupric ions in solution gain two electrons and are reduced to metallic copper, which electrodeposits onto the starter sheet or blank (US Patent 7736475, 2010): Cathode:  Cu²⁺ + 2e⁻ → Cu⁰        E° = +0.34 V At the anode, because the anode material is chosen to be chemically inert under the operating conditions, the anodic reaction is not dissolution of the electrode but the oxidation (decomposition) of water, releasing oxygen gas and regenerating sulfuric acid in the electrolyte (US Patent 7736475, 2010; Insoluble Titanium-Lead Anode, 2001): Anode:  H₂O → ½O₂ + 2H⁺ + 2e⁻        E° = –1.23 V The overall cell reaction is therefore the decomposition of copper sulfate and water into metallic copper, oxygen gas, and sulfuric acid, with a standard cell potential around –0.89 V (US Patent 7736475, 2010) — meaning the reaction is thermodynamically unfavourable and requires an externally applied voltage well above this value to proceed at a practical rate, mainly to overcome activation overpotential at the anode (oxygen evolution is kinetically sluggish), ohmic resistance of the electrolyte and busbars, and concentration polarisation of copper ions near the cathode surface (ScienceDirect, 2022a). 2.2 Faraday's Law and Deposition Rate The mass of copper deposited at the cathode is governed by Faraday's laws of electrolysis: the mass deposited is directly proportional to the total electrical charge passed and to the equivalent weight of the metal. For copper, a divalent ion (Cu²⁺), the theoretical mass deposited per unit charge is given by m = (I × t × M) / (n × F), where I is current (A), t is time (s), M is the atomic mass of copper (63.55 g/mol), n is the number of electrons transferred per ion (2), and F is Faraday's constant (96,485 C/mol). In practice, the actual mass deposited is somewhat less than this theoretical value because current efficiency is below 100%; industrial cells typically achieve 88–95% of the theoretical Faraday yield due to parasitic reactions and current losses (Molecules paper e-waste review, 2022; ResearchGate, 2022). This relationship is the basis for cathode weight and cycle-time calculations used in tankhouse design (911Metallurgist, no date a). 2.3 Energy Consumption and Comparison with Electrorefining Because the anode reaction is oxygen evolution rather than metal dissolution, electrowinning consumes considerably more electrical energy per unit of copper produced than electrorefining, where the anode is made of impure blister or fire-refined copper that dissolves anodically while pure copper deposits at the cathode — a reaction pair with a much smaller cell voltage (TSFX, no date). Typical specific energy consumption for copper electrowinning is on the order of 1,800–2,000 kWh per tonne of cathode copper (Ti-Better, 2025; Okon Recycling, 2025), compared with roughly 200–300 kWh per tonne for electrorefining, whose specific electrolytic energy consumption is reported at around 220 kWh per tonne of cathode copper under typical operating conditions (TSFX, no date; Okon Recycling, 2025). Electrorefining nonetheless produces the majority of the world's refined cathode copper (about 95%), with electrowinning contributing the remaining share (roughly 5% via the direct-electrolysis route, distinct from the ~20% attributable to the SX-EW hydrometallurgical pathway as a whole) (TSFX, no date). 2.4 Current Density, Current Efficiency, and Cell Voltage Industrial copper EW cells typically operate at current densities in the range of about 160–370 A/m² (Insoluble Titanium-Lead Anode, 2001), with modern high-productivity tankhouses trending toward current densities above 450 A/m² (ScienceDirect Topics, no date) and cell voltages typically around 2.0–2.2 V per cell (Ti-Better, 2025). Current efficiency — the fraction of the applied current that actually deposits copper rather than being lost to side reactions such as reduction of ferric ions that migrate from the anode compartment, short-circuiting between electrodes, or stray currents — is generally in the range of 88–95% in well-run plants (ScienceDirect, 2022b; 911Metallurgist, no date a). At Cerro Verde in Peru, for example, current efficiency progressed from 88% at start-up in 1977 through 90% following a 1990s expansion, illustrating how tankhouse optimisation improves this figure over a plant's operating life (911Metallurgist, no date a). 3. Electrowinning versus Electrorefining Electrolytic copper production takes two distinct forms, and the two are frequently confused because both use a DC current and a wet electrolyte to deposit pure copper on a cathode. In electrorefining, impure anodes cast from smelted (fire-refined/blister) copper are dissolved anodically while pure copper simultaneously deposits at the cathode; because the anode and cathode reactions are essentially the same half-reaction in reverse, the theoretical cell voltage is very small and energy consumption is correspondingly low, on the order of 220 kWh per tonne of cathode (TSFX, no date). In electrowinning, by contrast, the copper arrives in solution (from leaching and solvent extraction rather than from a cast anode) and the anode material does not dissolve; instead the anode sustains oxygen evolution from water, which requires a much larger applied voltage and results in energy consumption roughly six to nine times higher, at approximately 1,800–2,000 kWh per tonne (Ti-Better, 2025; Okon Recycling, 2025). Electrowinning Cell Design (Labelled Diagram) A copper electrowinning cell is a rectangular tank, historically built of concrete lined with plastic or rubber and increasingly constructed from polymer concrete, typically on the order of several metres long, roughly a metre to a metre and a quarter wide, and a little over a metre deep — Davenport et al. (2002), as cited by BCcampus Pressbooks (no date), give indicative dimensions of about 6 m long by 1.4 m deep by 1.25 m wide. Cells are electrically isolated from the ground/support structure to minimise stray-current losses (BCcampus Pressbooks, no date). Within each tank, flat rectangular anodes and cathodes are suspended vertically and alternated, spaced roughly 50 mm apart, with electrodes measuring on the order of 1 m × 1 m; all anodes and cathodes within a single tank are typically connected electrically in parallel, while successive tanks in the tankhouse are connected in series via busbars carrying current from a DC rectifier (Insoluble Titanium-Lead Anode, 2001). 4.1 Anode Materials The anodes are made of a lead alloy — commonly lead–calcium–tin or lead–antimony–calcium formulations chosen for corrosion resistance and mechanical stability under continuous oxygen evolution (Insoluble Titanium-Lead Anode, 2001) — or, increasingly in modern plants, of dimensionally stable, catalytically coated titanium, which can substantially cut energy consumption relative to conventional Pb-Ca-Sn-Cu anodes (US Patent 5622615, 1997, reports energy savings of approximately 42% for one such coated anode). A recognised drawback of lead alloy anodes is slow corrosion, which can transfer trace lead to the cathode deposit and requires periodic removal of accumulated lead sludge (mainly PbO2) from the cell floor (Insoluble Titanium-Lead Anode, 2001). 4.2 Cathode Materials The cathodes are either thin, pure-copper starter sheets or, in most modern operations, permanent stainless-steel “blanks” (typically 316L stainless steel) from which the deposited copper sheet is mechanically stripped once it reaches the target thickness (US Patent 11807952, 2023; ResearchGate, 2025b). The shift from copper starter sheets to permanent stainless-steel cathodes has been one of the most significant technological developments in the industry, reducing labour, improving cathode flatness and quality, and enabling automated stripping (ResearchGate, 2025b). The advance (copper-rich) electrolyte enters the cell from the solvent-extraction circuit, and the copper-depleted, acid-rich spent electrolyte overflows and is returned to solvent extraction to strip further copper from the loaded organic phase, closing the SX-EW loop (ScienceDirect Topics, no date). 4.4 Electrolyte Additives Cobalt sulfate is commonly added to the electrolyte, at concentrations on the order of 100–200 mg/L, to reduce anode corrosion and lead contamination of the cathode (911Metallurgist, no date c; US Patent 11807952, 2023), and organic “smoothing” agents such as guar gum are dosed, at consumption rates on the order of 200 g per tonne of cathode copper produced, to promote a level, adherent copper deposit (911Metallurgist, no date c). Overall Process Flow: Ore to Cathode (SX-EW) Copper electrowinning is normally the final stage of a three-step hydrometallurgical sequence: leaching, solvent extraction, and electrowinning (KGHM, no date). 5.1 Leaching Low-grade oxide, and increasingly some secondary sulfide, ores are stacked on lined heaps or dumps and irrigated with dilute sulfuric acid under atmospheric conditions, which dissolves copper minerals such as malachite, azurite, and chrysocolla to produce a dilute, impure pregnant leach solution, or PLS (KGHM, no date; 911Metallurgist, no date b). Historically, an alternative recovery method — cementation of copper from leach liquors onto scrap iron — was practised at scale from the eighteenth century onward, but this has been almost entirely superseded by SX-EW because electrowinning yields a directly saleable, high-purity cathode rather than an impure cement copper product requiring further processing (911Metallurgist, no date b). 5.2 Solvent Extraction (SX) The PLS is contacted with an organic extractant (typically a hydroxyoxime-type reagent) dissolved in a hydrocarbon diluent; the extractant selectively binds copper ions and transfers them into the organic phase, leaving impurities such as iron in the aqueous raffinate, which is recycled to leaching (SAIMM, 2002; Metso, no date b). The loaded organic is then contacted with a strong, copper-depleted acidic electrolyte returning from electrowinning; copper is stripped back into this aqueous phase, producing a clean, concentrated, high-purity copper sulfate “advance electrolyte” suitable for electrowinning (Metso, no date b). Suspended solids carried into the rich electrolyte at this stage — dirt, grease and other contaminants — can depress current efficiency and cathode purity in the subsequent electrowinning step, so solids removal is an important part of SX circuit design (US Patent 5733431, 1998). 5.3 Electrowinning (EW) The advance electrolyte is fed to the electrowinning tankhouse where, as described above, copper is electrodeposited on the cathode while oxygen and sulfuric acid are generated at the anode (Metso, no date b). The depleted electrolyte returns to solvent extraction to strip more copper, closing the circuit (ScienceDirect Topics, no date). Reference diagram — SX-EW flowsheet from pregnant leach solution to cathode: available for download from the Metso Copper SX-EW technology brochure — https://www.metso.com/globalassets/industry-pages/metals-refining/hydrometallurgy/mo_copper_sx-ew_technology_eng_lowres.pdf 5.4 Direct Electrowinning A related variant, direct electrowinning, feeds a leach solution to the tankhouse without an intervening solvent-extraction purification step (US Patent 6972107, 2005). It is simpler and avoids the capital cost of an SX circuit, but generally yields lower-purity cathode copper that often requires further refining or must be sold at a discount, because impurities that SX would otherwise remove report directly to the electrolyte and can co-deposit at the cathode (US Patent 6972107, 2005). For this reason SX-EW, rather than direct electrowinning, remains the dominant commercial configuration for hydrometallurgical copper cathode production (US Patent 6972107, 2005; US Patent 8012318, 2011). 6. Cathode Harvesting and Product Quality After a fixed deposition cycle — commonly five to nine days, after which the cathode is removed for stripping (Ti-Better, 2025) — cathodes are removed (harvested) from the cells, typically by overhead crane, and the electrodeposited copper sheet is mechanically stripped from the permanent stainless-steel blank, or, where copper starter sheets are used, the whole sheet is removed and later used as a cathode blank in electrorefining (ResearchGate, 2025b). Automated stripping machines are widely used in modern tankhouses to increase throughput and reduce manual handling and worker exposure to acidic conditions (ResearchGate, 2025b). Newly stripped cathodes are washed in warm water, at around 70°C, to remove residual electrolyte and adhering sulfur, although rewashing is sometimes needed because sulfur levels can remain elevated (ResearchGate, 2022b). Electrowon copper cathode is typically of very high purity, comparable to or exceeding electrorefined copper: its main residual impurities are sulfur, at around 4–5 ppm, and lead and iron, at around 1–2 ppm each, picked up from the anode and electrolyte (ScienceDirect Topics, no date). Careful housekeeping and tankhouse management can reduce these impurity levels toward the lower end of the typical ranges (ScienceDirect Topics, no date). LME- and industry-grade cathode specifications generally require copper purity of 99.99% or better, which well-controlled SX-EW cathode routinely meets (ScienceDirect Topics, no date). Operating Challenges: Impurities, Additives, and Acid Mist 8.1 Iron and Current Efficiency Losses Iron carried over from leaching (as Fe²⁺/Fe³⁺) is identified as one of the principal causes of current-efficiency loss in well-run plants: ferrous ion oxidised at the anode to ferric ion can migrate to the cathode and be reduced back to ferrous in a parasitic cycle that consumes current without depositing copper (ScienceDirect, 2022c). Jiang et al. (2024) demonstrate experimentally that elevated iron concentration also alters the structure of the protective lead-dioxide film that forms on the lead-alloy anode surface, changing both its corrosion resistance and its oxygen-evolution catalytic activity; in their tests, conducted at a current density of 300 A/m² with iron concentrations from 0 to 20 g/L, higher Fe³⁺ concentration was associated with reduced anode corrosion resistance even as oxygen-evolution activity improved. 8.2 Additives Cobalt sulfate is routinely dosed into the electrolyte because it promotes formation of a more protective, adherent PbO2 layer on the lead-alloy anode, reducing anode corrosion and the resulting contamination of cathode copper with lead (US Patent 11807952, 2023; 911Metallurgist, no date c). Organic colloids such as guar gum, together with compounds like thiourea, are added in small, carefully controlled doses — guar gum consumption of around 200 g per tonne of cathode copper is typical (911Metallurgist, no date c) — as levelling/brightening agents that promote smooth, dense cathode deposits and suppress dendritic or nodular growth that could cause short-circuits between electrodes (ResearchGate, 2022b). 8.3 Acid Mist Oxygen bubbles generated at the anode entrain droplets of the strongly acidic electrolyte and carry them into the air above the cells as the bubbles burst at the surface, producing a corrosive “acid mist” that is a recognised occupational health hazard, causing discomfort to the skin, eyes and respiratory system of tankhouse workers and historically contributing to high worker turnover (US Patent 5468353, 1995). Mist is controlled using floating plastic balls, mats or tube arrangements that provide a physical barrier at the electrolyte surface (US Patent 3948747, 1976; 911Metallurgist, no date c), chemical mist suppressants and surfactants — including specialty fluorochemicals and, more recently, natural extracts such as licorice-root-derived glycyrrhizin investigated as a synergistic mist suppressant with surfactants (US Patent 11807952, 2023) — as well as cell covers and tankhouse ventilation. 8.4 Anode and Cell Maintenance Slow corrosion of lead-alloy anodes generates lead sludge (mainly PbO2) that accumulates at the bottom of cells and must be periodically removed, since excessive build-up can be occluded into the cathode deposit and degrade cathode quality (ResearchGate, 2022b). At the KCM tankhouse, for example, cells are de-sludged on a 90-day cycle, and salt accumulation on hanger bars together with persistent acid mist have been noted as ongoing operational issues requiring protective equipment such as acid-resistant gloves, respirators and goggles for tankhouse workers (ResearchGate, 2022b). 9. Industrial Case Studies 9.1 Cerro Verde, Peru The Cerro Verde SX-EW plant in southwestern Peru, commissioned in April 1977, was the first solvent extraction and electrowinning plant built in Latin America and provides a well-documented illustration of how electrowinning practice has evolved over several decades of operation (911Metallurgist, no date a). At start-up, the tankhouse was designed to produce 33,000 tonnes per year of cathode copper at a current density of 184 A/m² and 88% current efficiency. Following privatisation and an expansion project in the 1990s, cathode production capacity increased to 48,000 tonnes per year at a current density of 250 A/m² and 90% current efficiency, and cathode output subsequently exceeded this design basis through process optimisation. By 2002, following further modifications to electrolyte flow, tenor and temperature control, electrode geometry, cell design and smoothing-agent practice, current densities at Cerro Verde had reached 368 A/m² (911Metallurgist, no date a). This progression — roughly doubling current density over 25 years while maintaining or improving current efficiency — exemplifies the industry-wide trend toward higher-productivity tankhouse operation described in Section 10 below. 9.2 KCM Tankhouse Operating data from the KCM electrowinning tankhouse illustrate typical day-to-day operating challenges: process variables such as electrolyte impurity levels, acid tenor, and additive (thiourea and cobalt sulfate) concentrations were found to influence cathode sulfur content, and practical issues including salt build-up on hanger bars and persistent acid mist were reported despite the tankhouse being covered (ResearchGate, 2022b). Such case-level operating data underline that, alongside the underlying electrochemistry, tankhouse housekeeping and process control are decisive for consistent cathode quality. 10. Recent Advances and Future Trends 10.1 Alternative Anode Reactions Because conventional oxygen-evolving anodes account for most of the excess cell voltage — and hence most of the energy cost — of electrowinning relative to electrorefining, a major strand of research and patent activity has focused on replacing the water-oxidation anode reaction with a lower-voltage alternative. One approach substitutes a ferrous/ferric (Fe²⁺/Fe³⁺) redox couple for oxygen evolution at the anode, which patent literature reports can allow cells to be operated at voltages below about 1.5 V and current densities above roughly 26 A/ft² (US Patent 7378011, 2008). A related route uses sulfur-dioxide-depolarised (SDD) anodes; modelling reported in one patent indicates that platinum-coated titanium electrodes used with SDD electrowinning can substantially reduce overall cell voltage and standard electrical energy consumption relative to conventional PbCaSn or iridium/tantalum-oxide-coated dimensionally stable anodes (US Patent 9932683, 2018). A further variant electrowins copper directly from copper-matte-derived cuprous solutions, in which the electrochemical process operates without oxygen evolution at the anode and, despite current densities three to seven times higher than conventional copper electrowinning, achieves energy consumption at or below that of conventional practice, on the order of 1.2 kWh/kg (US Patent 5622615, 1997). 10.2 Anode Coatings and Materials Dimensionally stable anodes coated with mixed metal oxides (for example, iridium oxide/tantalum oxide on titanium) are increasingly used in place of, or alongside, traditional lead alloys, and pilot-scale testing has shown that current distribution to the cathode is more uniform with such coated titanium anodes than with conventional lead-alloy anodes (ResearchGate, 2022a). 10.3 Permanent Cathode Technology and Automation The replacement of copper starter-sheet cathodes with permanent stainless-steel (or titanium) cathode blanks has been described as one of the most significant developments in modern copper electrowinning practice, since it removes the need to cast and handle copper starter sheets and enables mechanised, automated stripping (ResearchGate, 2025b). Modern tankhouse design increasingly incorporates robotic and automated equipment for cathode harvesting, stripping and quality inspection, reducing labour requirements and worker exposure to acid mist and heavy lifting (ScienceDirect Topics, no date; ResearchGate, 2025b). 10.4 Higher Current Densities A consistent trend across modern tankhouses is a push toward higher current densities — above 450 A/m² in leading operations — enabled by improved electrolyte circulation and flow design, better anode/cathode geometry and spacing, and more effective additive regimes, alongside continuing efforts to reduce energy consumption and increase automation and robotic monitoring of tankhouse operations (ScienceDirect Topics, no date). 11. Industrial Significance and Applications Recovery of copper from low-grade oxide and secondary sulfide ores that are uneconomical to concentrate and smelt, extending the economic life of mineral deposits via heap leaching combined with SX-EW (Bartos, 2002). Production of very high-purity (≥99.99%) cathode copper suitable for direct use in wire rod, tube, and other electrical/thermal conductivity applications without further electrorefining (ScienceDirect Topics, no date). Recovery of copper from secondary and waste streams, including electronic-waste (e-waste) leach liquors and acid mine drainage, as a more sustainable alternative to primary mining; direct electrowinning of copper from acid mine drainage using porous copper sheet or carbon-felt cathodes has been reported to achieve copper removal rates above 92% (PSU ETDA, no date). A lower direct-process-emission alternative to pyrometallurgical smelting: the hydrometallurgical SX-EW route does not require high-temperature roasting or smelting and does not directly generate the greenhouse gases associated with pyrometallurgical reactions, and the overall hydrometallurgical pathway can have a substantially lower carbon footprint — reportedly up to three times lower in tCO2e per tonne of copper in some cases — than the pyrometallurgical route, although the emissions associated with electrowinning itself still depend heavily on how the electricity used was generated (CarbonChain, 2023). 12. Environmental and Economic Considerations Because the electrowinning anode reaction is oxygen evolution rather than metal dissolution, electrowinning is inherently more energy-intensive per tonne of copper than electrorefining (TSFX, no date), and electricity cost is consequently one of the largest operating cost components of an SX-EW operation (Ti-Better, 2025). Ongoing industrial and research efforts to reduce this cost include the alternative, lower-voltage anode reactions discussed in Section 10 (ferrous/ferric or sulfur-dioxide-depolarised anode chemistry in place of oxygen evolution), improved dimensionally stable anode coatings, and higher current densities enabled by better electrolyte flow and cell design (US Patent 7378011, 2008; US Patent 9932683, 2018; ScienceDirect Topics, no date). CarbonChain (2023) notes that electrowinning does not require high process temperatures and does not directly produce greenhouse gases through its chemical reactions, and that solutions can be reused at each stage of the hydrometallurgical circuit; nevertheless, as an electricity-intensive process, its indirect emissions are determined largely by the carbon intensity of the grid or generation source supplying the tankhouse rectifiers, making low-carbon or renewable electricity supply an important lever for reducing the greenhouse gas footprint of electrowon copper (CarbonChain, 2023). Occupational health and environmental management — particularly acid mist control and lead sludge management, discussed in Section 8 — are also material components of the operating cost and regulatory compliance burden of an electrowinning tankhouse (US Patent 5468353, 1995; ResearchGate, 2022b). 13. Summary Copper electrowinning is an electrolytic process that reduces dissolved cupric ions to metallic copper at a cathode while an inert anode sustains oxygen evolution and acid regeneration, all driven by an externally applied DC potential across a cell of alternating lead-alloy (or coated titanium) anodes and stainless-steel or copper cathodes. Since the first commercial SX-EW plant began operating in 1968 (SAIMM, 2002), the technology has grown from a niche complement to conventional smelting into a major route supplying roughly a fifth of the world's copper (Wikipedia, no date), and it remains the only practical means of converting a dilute, dissolved leach solution directly into high-purity, saleable cathode copper without an intervening smelting step. Its principal technical challenges — the inherently higher energy intensity of oxygen-evolving anodes compared with electrorefining, current-efficiency losses from iron and other impurities, acid mist generation, and anode/cell maintenance — continue to be the focus of active process improvement, including alternative anode chemistries, permanent cathode technology, automation, and higher current densities, as illustrated by long-running operations such as Cerro Verde. As demand for copper grows alongside electrification and renewable-energy deployment, and as more of that copper is expected to come from lower-grade and secondary/recycled sources, electrowinning is likely to remain an increasingly important part of the global copper supply chain.
